\begin{center}\large\bfseries Abstract\end{center}
\begin{quote}\small
\citet{kantamneni2025helix} (K\&T) show that LLMs encode integers
$a \in [0,99]$ as a \emph{generalised helix}: a linear ``number-line''
axis plus modular circles at $T \in \{2,5,10,100\}$. A natural reading
is that the transformer constructs this geometry through depth. We
introduce a four-part diagnostic kit that asks where the geometry
\emph{comes from}: (i)~the provenance ratio
$\rho = R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L\!=\!0) / \max_L R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L)$,
(ii)~a basis-bandwidth and window-wrap check, (iii)~a random-embedding
control, and (iv)~CKA between the L=0 and peak-layer helix subspaces.
Applied across 8 models $\times$ 12 numeral scripts (96 cells), the
kit shows that K\&T's central Latin result holds cleanly on their
three models (Pythia-6.9B, Llama-3.1-8B, GPT-J-6B; $\rho = 0.34, 0.39,
0.41$) but that the apparent cross-script generalisation of the helix
is largely \emph{pre-transformer}: only $6{-}8$ of $96$ cells clear a
joint ($\rho$, quality) bar for depth-built geometry. The clearest
single result is on Babylonian cuneiform: replacing every learned
embedding with a random vector at matched scale leaves the L=0 helix
score unchanged on all 8 models ($|\Delta R^2| \le 0.008$ in the
native protocol). The measured base-60 geometry arises mechanically
from how the renderer encodes wedges and how mean-pooling combines
them, with no learned semantics required. Probes, SAEs, and any
``the model represents X as a manifold'' claim need a provenance
check before attributing measured geometry to learning.
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{EleutherAI__pythia-6.9b/latin/mean/fig1_helix_T10.png}
\caption{The number helix at $T=10$ in Pythia-6.9B's residual stream,
  Latin digits 0--99, peak layer. Two axes are the
  $(\cos 2\pi a/10, \sin 2\pi a/10)$ pair; the vertical axis is the
  linear ``number-line'' direction. Colour indicates $a$. Integers
  sharing the units digit land at the same angular position;
  magnitude lifts them along the vertical.}
\label{fig:helix-intro}
\end{figure}

\citet{kantamneni2025helix} reverse-engineer a striking representation:
in the residual stream of three mid-sized LLMs (Pythia-6.9B, GPT-J-6B,
Llama-3.1-8B), each integer $a \in [0,99]$ is encoded as a generalised
helix---a linear magnitude axis plus orthogonal modular circles at
periods $T \in \{2, 5, 10, 100\}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:helix-intro} shows the
$T=10$ slice). The basis is itself the LLM-scale echo of
\citet{nanda2023progress}, who reverse-engineered the trig solution
in a tiny transformer trained on modular addition (itself an
interpretation of the \citet{power2022grokking} grokking dynamics).
K\&T further show the helix is causally used for addition.

A high helix $R^2$ does \emph{not} tell us where the geometry came from.
It might be constructed by transformer depth, or inherited from the
input pipeline (rendering, tokenization, embedding lookup, pooling),
or induced by the measurement protocol itself (wrong basis, too-short
input range). This question matters: in the literature, ``the model
represents $X$ as a [helix/sphere/manifold]'' is routinely read as a
claim about what the transformer does, when it can in principle be a
claim about how the inputs were prepared.

This provenance question is also motivated by behavioral work on
number tokenization. \citet{singh2024tokenization} show that
arithmetic performance in frontier LLMs depends strongly on how
numbers are tokenized, with systematic error patterns at token
boundaries. Their result is behavioral; ours is representational.
If tokenization can change arithmetic behavior, can the input
pipeline also \emph{create} the geometry we later interpret as a
learned representation?

\textbf{Contributions.} We introduce a four-part diagnostic kit
(\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}) and apply it across 8 models $\times$ 12
numeral scripts (\S\ref{sec:setup}). We report three findings
(\S\ref{sec:results}). \emph{(1)} K\&T's Pythia/Llama/GPT-J Latin
cells survive cleanly under the wider protocol; the depth-built
finding holds where the original paper claimed it. \emph{(2)} The
``helix-in-script-$X$-too'' generalisation is mostly pre-transformer:
only $6{-}8$ of $96$ cells qualify as depth-built or depth-amplified
under a joint $\rho$+quality criterion. \emph{(3)} Babylonian
cuneiform is the cleanest case of mechanically-produced geometry:
swapping every learned embedding for a random vector leaves the L=0
helix score unchanged on all 8 models. We discuss implications for
probing and SAE interpretability (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

Complementary causal work: \citet{feucht2026wild} identify a layer-18
base-10 addition module in Llama-3.1-8B that reads from exactly the
Fourier features the helix basis fits, on exactly the cell our
diagnostic marks as depth-built---answering the
\emph{what is the geometry used for?} question on a cell we mark as
the geometry-was-actually-built one.

\section{The Diagnostic Kit}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

We characterise a $(\text{model}, \text{script})$ cell along four
axes. Let $H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be the residual stream at
layer $L$ over $n$ integers, with $h(a)$ the row for integer $a$.

\paragraph{Helix basis and quality.} The trig basis is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:basis}
B(a) = \big[\,a,\, \cos(2\pi a/T), \sin(2\pi a/T)\,\big]_{T \in \mathcal{T}}.
\end{equation}
We fit $H \approx B W$ by ordinary least squares and report the
variance-weighted $R^2$ at every layer, plus the K-d PCA upper
bound at the same dimensionality (Eckart--Young). The ratio
$R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}}$ measures \emph{quality}:
how dominant the helix subspace is among all K-d fits.

\paragraph{Provenance ratio $\rho$.} The core diagnostic:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rho}
\rho \;=\; \frac{R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L\!=\!0)}{\max_L R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L)}.
\end{equation}
$L=0$ is the residual stream \emph{before any transformer block has
run}---the output of token embedding, positional embedding, and
mean-pooling over numeral sub-tokens. $\rho = 1$ means the helix is
already present at L=0 and depth does not improve the score (inherited);
$\rho \to 0$ means depth substantially builds it (depth-built). $\rho$
alone is insufficient: a low $\rho$ in a low-quality cell just means
the basis fits little anywhere. We classify a cell as
\emph{depth-built} when $\rho \le 0.55$ \emph{and}
$R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}} \ge 0.70$; \emph{inherited}
when $\rho \ge 0.80$ at quality $\ge 0.55$; \emph{weak} when quality
$< 0.55$; \emph{ambiguous} otherwise.

\paragraph{Basis bandwidth and window wrap.} A trig basis can only
fit periods it includes; a fitted period $T$ requires the input window
to wrap $T$ several times. Babylonian (native $T=60$) and hex
(natural $T \in \{16,32,64,256\}$) fail both bars at the paper
defaults. Mitigation: widen the window past several wraps and add
the native period to $\mathcal{T}$.

\paragraph{Random-embedding control.} Replace the model's learned
embedding matrix with $E \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{d})$ at matched
scale, recompute the L=0 representation (rendering + tokenization +
mean-pooling are unchanged), and re-fit the helix. A small
$\Delta R^2_{L=0} = R^2_{\mathrm{learned}} - R^2_{\mathrm{random}}$
means the L=0 geometry is mechanical---the embedding table contributed
nothing the renderer + pooling did not already produce.

\paragraph{Representation alignment (CKA).} Linear CKA
\citep{kornblith2019similarity} between the helix-projected L=0 and
peak-layer representations distinguishes two cases $\rho$ conflates:
\emph{pass-through} (high $\rho$, high CKA) versus \emph{rebuild}
(high $\rho$, low CKA: depth produces a similar-quality helix with a
substantially different integer-to-integer geometry). CKA is
rotation- and rescaling-invariant.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Models} (all base, non-instruction-tuned): Pythia-6.9B,
GPT-J-6B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-4-E4B, Gemma-4-31B, OLMo-3-32B,
Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B. Three of these (Pythia, GPT-J, Llama) are
the original K\&T set.

\textbf{Scripts} (12 total, four families); Table~\ref{tab:scripts}
gives each script's rendering of 23 and its mathematical family.
All inputs use mean pooling over numeral sub-tokens (last-token
pooling produces a period-10 artifact for multi-token scripts; see
App.~\ref{app:methods}). The default protocol is $n_{\max}=100$,
$\mathcal{T}=\{2,5,10,100\}$; Babylonian/binary/hex additionally run
with wider windows and native bases (App.~\ref{app:bases}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The paper-models / Latin cells survive cleanly}

On K\&T's three models on Latin, $\rho$ is well below
the inherited threshold and quality is high: Pythia $\rho=0.34$
($R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}}=0.85$), Llama $\rho=0.39$
($0.85$), GPT-J $\rho=0.41$ ($0.83$). Less than half of the helix is
at L=0; depth substantially builds it. These three cells are
unambiguously depth-built under our criterion.

\subsection{Cross-script generalisation is mostly pre-transformer}
\label{sec:results-cross}

Figure~\ref{fig:rho-heatmap} shows $\rho$ for all 96 cells at
paper-default protocol. The cool (depth-built) row is narrow: outside
the three paper-model Latin cells, depth substantially contributes
only on Gemma's strong non-Latin cells (Devanagari, Persian, Roman;
$\rho = 0.44{-}0.63$). Most non-Latin cells on Pythia and Llama sit
at $\rho = 0.73{-}1.00$: the helix is already present at L=0 because
the tokenizer splits e.g.\ \arabicnum{٢٣} into per-digit sub-tokens
whose mean varies smoothly with digit values, which the trig basis
fits. Qwen takes pre-transformer geometry to its limit even on Latin:
Qwen2.5-7B/Latin has $\rho = 1.00$ (helix peaks at L=0 exactly).
Greek alphabetic numerals are universally weak ($\rho \ge 0.94$ on
every non-Gemma model). The mutually-exclusive classifier
(\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}) marks 6 of 96 cells as depth-built and 4
as depth-amplified at paper defaults; the full grid is in
App.~\ref{app:classification}.

\subsection{Babylonian: geometry from rendering alone}
\label{sec:results-bab}

Babylonian cuneiform is the cleanest mechanical case. The natural
protocol fix---widen $n_{\max}$ to 600 and add $T=60$ to
$\mathcal{T}$---recovers $8{-}16$ percentage points of helix $R^2$
uniformly across all 8 models. But the random-embedding control
shows this recovered geometry is not learned. Table~\ref{tab:bab-rand}
gives the learned-vs-random $\Delta R^2_{L=0}$ on the native protocol;
the maximum absolute gap is $0.0073$. The L=0 Babylonian helix is
mechanical: cuneiform renders $n$ as additive wedge counts
($\text{n}{=}23 \to$ \cuneinum{𒌋𒌋𒁹𒁹𒁹}), the tokenizer splits
those wedges, and mean-pooling produces a vector
$h(a) \approx (t \cdot e_{\text{ten}} + u \cdot e_{\text{one}})/(t{+}u)$
that varies smoothly with $a$ for \emph{any} embedding vectors. The
Fourier basis fits the smooth variation. This recapitulates the
representational analogue of \citet{singh2024tokenization}:
tokenization is not a neutral preprocessing detail but an inductive
bias in the numerical reasoning pipeline.

\subsection{Pass-through versus rebuild}
\label{sec:results-cka}

$\rho$ alone conflates two mechanistically distinct cases that CKA
separates. \emph{Pass-through}: Qwen2.5-32B/Latin ($\rho = 0.89$,
$\text{CKA} = 0.91$) and OLMo/Latin ($0.63$, $0.91$)---depth leaves
the row-similarity geometry untouched. \emph{Rebuild}:
Gemma/Latin ($\rho \approx 0.85$, $\text{CKA} \approx 0.34$)---depth
produces a similar-quality helix with a substantially different
integer-to-integer geometry. On Babylonian, Pythia/Llama/OLMo have
$\rho \approx 0.86$ but CKA $= 0.25{-}0.53$, so the peak Babylonian
helix is depth-built on top of a mechanical L=0 starting point. The
full $(\rho, \text{CKA})$ scatter is in App.~\ref{app:cka}.

\subsection{Binary and hex (one-paragraph summary)}

Two more mode-1 case studies, two different stories. Binary ($n=1024$,
$\mathcal{T} = \{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$) is mechanical like Babylonian:
the random-embedding gap is $|\Delta| \le 0.03$ on every model. Hex
($n=1024$, $\mathcal{T}=\{16,32,64,256\}$) is depth-built on the
paper models: native-basis $\rho = 0.29, 0.30, 0.35, 0.35$ on
Pythia/Llama/GPT-J/OLMo, and the random-embedding gap is positive on
every model. The natural hypothesis (hex exposure in pre-training
code/addresses/hashes) is not directly tested by our data. Full
native-basis tables in App.~\ref{app:bases}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The contribution here is the diagnostic kit, not the matrix. Three
problems can make the standard ``fit a helix, report $R^2$''
procedure answer the wrong question: two hide real structure (wrong
basis, too-short window), one credits the transformer with structure
it did not build ($\rho$ catches this), and CKA reveals a fourth---a
high $\rho$ does not always mean the geometry is untouched, sometimes
depth rebuilds the integer-to-integer pattern with similar quality
but different shape. The cells where K\&T's reading---``depth builds
the helix''---is unambiguously correct are narrower than the
generalisation suggests: 6 of 96 at paper defaults, concentrated on
the three paper-model Latin cells and Gemma's strong non-Latin
scripts.

The Babylonian random-embedding result is the sharpest version of a
general warning: \emph{some apparent neural geometry can come from
rendering statistics alone}, before learned embeddings or transformer
blocks are needed. Smooth, low-dimensional, easy-to-fit manifolds can
be artefacts of how you encode the input. For number helices, $\rho
+ \text{CKA}$ + the two spectral checks are the cheapest version of
the provenance question; for other geometric claims (probe-discovered
features, SAE directions, ``the model represents $X$ as a manifold''),
analogues are needed before attributing measured geometry to learning.

The diagnostic does not invalidate the literature: K\&T's Latin
results survive cleanly, and the replication across eight models
shows the helix is architecturally general. The Babylonian base-60
result is then a substantive statement about input pipelines, even
if not about transformers. Causal work like \citet{feucht2026wild}
answers the follow-up \emph{what does the model do with the
geometry?} on exactly the cells our diagnostic marks as
depth-built.

\section{Methods details}
\label{app:methods}

\paragraph{Activation collection.} For each integer $a$, we feed
``\texttt{<bos> {\normalfont format\_number}($a$, script)}'' through
the model with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True} and pool the
numeral's sub-token positions at every layer. We use mean pooling
throughout; last-token pooling produces a period-10 artefact for
multi-token scripts because numbers $23, 33, 43, \ldots$ all share
the same last sub-token in per-digit BPE.

\paragraph{Helix fit.} For numbers $\{0, \ldots, n_{\max}-1\}$ and
periods $\mathcal{T}$, we construct $B$ of shape
$(n_{\max}, 1 + 2|\mathcal{T}|)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:basis}, then solve
$H \approx B W$ by ordinary least squares with intercept. Report
variance-weighted $R^2$. The Eckart--Young upper bound is the
variance-weighted reconstruction $R^2$ of the best $(1 + 2|\mathcal{T}|)$-d
PCA of $H$; the helix/PCA ratio is our quality metric.

\paragraph{$\rho$ and the peak layer.} We collect activations at every
\texttt{hidden\_states[L]} (one forward pass per $a$), so $L=0$ is the
embedding output (after the token + positional embeddings and pooling,
before any transformer block). We compute the helix $R^2$ at every
$L$ and define $\rho$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:rho}.

\paragraph{Random-embedding control.} For a model with embedding
matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}$ we draw
$\tilde{E} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{d})$ (matched per-coordinate
scale), use the model's actual tokenizer to obtain sub-token id
sequences for each $a$, mean-pool $\tilde{E}$ rows over those
sub-tokens to obtain $\tilde{h}(a)$, fit the helix on $\tilde{H}$, and
compare $\tilde{R}^2$ to the learned $R^2_{L=0}$. The tokenizer +
renderer + pooling are identical to the learned condition; only the
embedding lookup is replaced. No transformer blocks are run.

\paragraph{Linear CKA.} For mean-centred $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_x}$,
$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_y}$,
$\mathrm{CKA}(X,Y) = \|X^\top Y\|_F^2 \,/\, (\|X^\top X\|_F \cdot \|Y^\top Y\|_F).$
We report CKA on the helix-projected representations
$X = B W^{(L\!=\!0)}$, $Y = B W^{(\text{peak})}$, isolating whether
the \emph{helix slice} of the residual stream is the same at the two
depths, not whether the rest of the model state is.

\paragraph{Classification thresholds.} Mutually exclusive criteria:
\emph{depth-built} requires $\rho \le 0.55$ \emph{and} helix/PCA $\ge 0.70$;
\emph{depth-amplified} requires $0.55 < \rho \le 0.70$ and helix/PCA
$\ge 0.60$; \emph{inherited} requires $\rho \ge 0.80$ at helix/PCA
$\ge 0.55$; \emph{weak} is helix/PCA $< 0.55$; \emph{ambiguous} is
everything else (e.g.\ $0.70 < \rho < 0.80$). The bar is deliberately
strict; relaxing helix/PCA to $0.60$ adds Gemma's two Roman cells.

\section{Per-model peak layers on Latin}
\label{app:layers}

\textbf{Where the helix lives varies dramatically across models.}
Pythia ($n_L = 32$) rises monotonically and peaks at the literal
final layer. GPT-J ($n_L = 28$) does the same. Llama-3.1-8B ($n_L
= 32$) peaks mid-stack at L=15. Gemma-4-E4B ($n_L = 42$) shows two
regimes with a broad peak at L=17. Gemma-4-31B ($n_L = 60$) holds
$\sim 0.5$ across most of the stack with a sharp spike to $0.70$ at
L=29. OLMo-3-32B ($n_L = 64$) rises through mid-stack to a peak at
L=23. Qwen2.5-7B/Latin peaks at the literal embedding output
(L=0; the helix is complete \emph{before any block has run}).
Qwen2.5-32B peaks at L=3 of 64. ``Read the middle layer'' is a
Pythia-ism; for any new model, a layer sweep is mandatory before
defining $\rho$.

\section{$(\rho, \text{CKA})$ scatter and the four quadrants}
\label{app:cka}

We compute linear CKA between the helix-projected L=0 representation
and the helix-projected peak-layer representation for every cell. Four
quadrants emerge in the $(\rho, \text{CKA})$ plane.

\textbf{Pass-through} (high $\rho$, high CKA): Qwen2.5-32B/Latin
($\rho=0.89$, $\text{CKA}=0.91$), OLMo/Latin ($0.63$, $0.91$),
Pythia $\times$ Persian/Devanagari/Chinese ($\text{CKA} \ge 0.98$).
\textbf{Depth refines} (low-to-moderate $\rho$, high CKA):
Pythia/Latin ($0.34$, $0.79$), Llama/Latin ($0.39$, $0.81$), Roman on
every non-Gemma model ($\text{CKA} = 0.77{-}0.90$).
\textbf{Rebuild} (high $\rho$, low CKA): the entire Gemma family
($\text{CKA} = 0.21{-}0.65$ across most scripts), Babylonian on
Pythia/Llama/OLMo ($\rho \approx 0.86$, $\text{CKA} = 0.25{-}0.53$),
Qwen on Devanagari ($\text{CKA} \approx 0.24{-}0.30$).
\textbf{Depth builds new} (low $\rho$, low CKA): rare; Gemma's
Devanagari/Persian/Roman/Greek cells edge into it
($\rho \approx 0.44{-}0.63$, $\text{CKA} \approx 0.29{-}0.57$).

The pass-through / rebuild distinction is the key new fact CKA
contributes. The simpler reading---``Gemma/Latin $\rho \approx 0.85$,
so mostly tokenizer''---was wrong. CKA on the same cell is only
$0.33$; depth produces a similar-quality helix with a substantially
different integer-to-integer geometry. Across nearly every script,
Gemma rebuilds. $\rho$ alone made it look like Gemma was doing
nothing; it is doing a lot, just not in a way that changes the score.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

All code is public at
\url{https://github.com/girish/addition_helix} (final URL on
publication). Each driver script declares its dependencies via PEP 723
inline metadata and is runnable directly via \texttt{uv run}; one
top-level \texttt{run.sh} executes the per-cell sweep
(\texttt{main.py}), the aggregator (\texttt{aggregate.py}), the
random-embedding control (\texttt{embed\_control.py}), and the
representation-alignment pass (\texttt{subspace\_align.py}). The
companion blogpost site (\texttt{www/}) reads the aggregated JSON
and renders all figures interactively.

\paragraph{Compute.} The full 96-cell sweep plus the three auxiliary
passes completes in 6--8 hours on a single Apple M5 Max with 128\,GB
unified memory (MPS); fallback CUDA/CPU paths are in
\texttt{main.py:pick\_device}. The 32B-class models (Gemma-4-31B,
OLMo-3-32B, Qwen2.5-32B) run in bf16. Each combination is idempotent:
re-running skips any cell whose \texttt{fig\_layer\_sweep.json}
already exists.

\paragraph{Data.} All inputs are integers; we render them via
\texttt{format\_number} in \texttt{helix\_lib.py} (12 scripts) and
feed them through each model's own tokenizer. No external corpus is
used.
