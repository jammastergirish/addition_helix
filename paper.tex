\begin{center}\large\bfseries Abstract\end{center}
\begin{quote}\small
\citet{kantamneni2025helix} (K\&T) show that LLMs encode integers
$a \in [0,99]$ as a \emph{generalised helix}: a linear ``number-line''
axis plus modular circles at $T \in \{2,5,10,100\}$. A natural reading
is that the transformer constructs this geometry through depth. We
ask where the geometry \emph{comes from}, and report four measurements
per cell: (i)~the provenance ratio
$\rho = R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L\!=\!0) / \max_L R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L)$,
(ii)~a basis-bandwidth and window-wrap check, (iii)~a random-embedding
control, and (iv)~CKA between the L=0 and peak-layer helix subspaces.
Across 8 models $\times$ 12 numeral scripts (96 cells), K\&T's central
Latin result holds cleanly on their
three models (Pythia-6.9B, Llama-3.1-8B, GPT-J-6B; $\rho = 0.34, 0.39,
0.41$) but that the apparent cross-script generalisation of the helix
is largely \emph{pre-transformer}: only $6{-}8$ of $96$ cells clear a
joint ($\rho$, quality) bar for depth-built geometry. The clearest
single result is on Babylonian cuneiform: replacing every learned
embedding with a random vector at matched scale leaves the L=0 helix
score unchanged on all 8 models ($|\Delta R^2| \le 0.008$ in the
native protocol). The measured base-60 geometry arises mechanically
from how the renderer encodes wedges and how mean-pooling combines
them, with no learned semantics required. Probes, SAEs, and any
``the model represents X as a manifold'' claim need a provenance
check before attributing measured geometry to learning.
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{EleutherAI__pythia-6.9b/latin/mean/fig1_helix_T10.png}
\caption{The number helix at $T=10$ in Pythia-6.9B's residual stream,
  Latin digits 0--99, peak layer. Two axes are the
  $(\cos 2\pi a/10, \sin 2\pi a/10)$ pair; the vertical axis is the
  linear ``number-line'' direction. Colour indicates $a$. Integers
  sharing the units digit land at the same angular position;
  magnitude lifts them along the vertical.}
\label{fig:helix-intro}
\end{figure}

\citet{kantamneni2025helix} reverse-engineer a striking representation:
in the residual stream of three mid-sized LLMs (Pythia-6.9B, GPT-J-6B,
Llama-3.1-8B), each integer $a \in [0,99]$ is encoded as a generalised
helix---a linear magnitude axis plus orthogonal modular circles at
periods $T \in \{2, 5, 10, 100\}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:helix-intro} shows the
$T=10$ slice). The basis is itself the LLM-scale echo of
\citet{nanda2023progress}, who reverse-engineered the trig solution
in a tiny transformer trained on modular addition (itself an
interpretation of the \citet{power2022grokking} grokking dynamics).
K\&T further show the helix is causally used for addition.

K\&T deliberately avoid many tokenization complications by focusing
on space-prefixed Latin integers that are single tokens in their
models---GPT-J tokenizes $[0, 361]$ as one token, Pythia $[0, 557]$,
Llama $[0, 999]$---which is the right clean setting for their causal
addition study. Our question starts where that simplification stops.
When the same helical fit is generalised across scripts and bases,
the input pipeline itself can create or preserve Fourier-fittable
structure, so provenance becomes a non-trivial question. A high
helix $R^2$ does \emph{not} tell us where the geometry came from. It
might be constructed by transformer depth, or inherited from the
input pipeline (rendering, tokenization, embedding lookup, pooling),
or induced by the measurement protocol itself (wrong basis, too-short
input range). This question matters: in the literature, ``the model
represents $X$ as a [helix/sphere/manifold]'' is routinely read as a
claim about what the transformer does, when it can in principle be a
claim about how the inputs were prepared.

This provenance question is also motivated by behavioral work on
number tokenization. \citet{singh2024tokenization} show that
arithmetic performance in frontier LLMs depends strongly on how
numbers are tokenized, with systematic error patterns at token
boundaries. Their result is behavioral; ours is representational.
If tokenization can change arithmetic behavior, can the input
pipeline also \emph{create} the geometry we later interpret as a
learned representation?

\textbf{Contributions.} We describe four measurements
(\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}) and what they say across 8 models $\times$ 12
numeral scripts (\S\ref{sec:setup}). We report three findings
(\S\ref{sec:results}). \emph{(1)} K\&T's Pythia/Llama/GPT-J Latin
cells survive cleanly under the wider protocol; the depth-built
finding holds where the original paper claimed it. \emph{(2)} The
``helix-in-script-$X$-too'' generalisation is mostly pre-transformer:
only $6{-}8$ of $96$ cells qualify as depth-built or depth-amplified
under a joint $\rho$+quality criterion. \emph{(3)} Babylonian
cuneiform is the cleanest case of mechanically-produced geometry:
swapping every learned embedding for a random vector leaves the L=0
helix score unchanged on all 8 models. We discuss implications for
probing and SAE interpretability (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

Complementary causal work: \citet{feucht2026wild} identify a layer-18
base-10 addition module in Llama-3.1-8B that reads from exactly the
Fourier features the helix basis fits, on exactly the cell our
measurements mark as depth-built---answering the
\emph{what is the geometry used for?} question on a cell we mark as
the geometry-was-actually-built one.

\section{What We Measure Per Cell}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

A high helix $R^2$ at the best layer is a measurement; on its own it
doesn't tell us how the geometry got there. To answer the provenance
question we report four separate measurements per
$(\text{model}, \text{script})$ cell: (i)~the helix score and how
dominant the helix subspace is among all $K$-d fits; (ii)~where in
the stack the helix actually lives, and how much of it is already at
the embedding output; (iii)~whether the basis and window can in
principle see the script's natural period; and (iv)~when depth does
change the score, whether it preserves the geometry or replaces it.
Let $H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be the residual stream at layer
$L$ over $n$ integers, with $h(a)$ the row for integer $a$.

\paragraph{Helix basis and quality.} The trig basis is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:basis}
B(a) = \big[\,a,\, \cos(2\pi a/T), \sin(2\pi a/T)\,\big]_{T \in \mathcal{T}},
\end{equation}
giving $1 + 2|\mathcal{T}|$ features per integer (9 features at
$\mathcal{T} = \{2,5,10,100\}$). Each column has a geometric meaning.
The linear column $a$ is the ``spine''; when OLS fits it, the column
picks out the residual-stream direction that best correlates with
integer magnitude---the number-line direction. Each
$(\cos 2\pi a/T, \sin 2\pi a/T)$ pair traces the unit circle as $a$
varies, completing one revolution per $T$ integers, so integers
sharing the same $(a \bmod T)$ land at the same angular position.
$T=2$ separates parity, $T=5$ gives five clusters by mod-5, $T=10$
gives the canonical clock face for the units digit, $T=100$ gives
coarse position over the whole 0--99 range. Stacked, the result is
a helix: the cos/sin pair rotates as $a$ increases and the linear
column lifts each point. We keep cos and sin separated (rather than
using the complex exponential $e^{2\pi i a/T}$) because the fit is
real-valued OLS and because each (cos, sin) pair gives the model a
2D plane it can \emph{rotate in}---which is exactly the ``Clock''
algorithm K\&T identify, where addition becomes rotation by the
addend's angle on each clock.

We fit $H \approx B W$ by ordinary least squares and report the
variance-weighted $R^2$ at every layer. The variance weighting puts
emphasis on the hidden dimensions that actually vary in $a$;
otherwise the score would be dominated by the many residual-stream
dimensions that are nearly constant across integers (tokens for
``language'', ``decimal'', etc.). The columns of $W$ tell us
\emph{where} each clock and the number-line direction live in
residual space---and Figure~\ref{fig:helix-intro} is constructed by
extracting the three columns
$(\cos 2\pi a/10, \sin 2\pi a/10, a)$, QR-orthonormalising them into
a clean 3D frame, and projecting $H$ onto it.

A high helix $R^2$ alone is not enough: any sufficiently expressive
basis fits a low-dimensional signal. The relevant comparison is the
K-d PCA upper bound at the same dimensionality (the Eckart--Young
optimal $K$-feature reconstruction). The ratio
$R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}}$ is our \emph{quality}
metric: how dominant the helix subspace is among all $K$-d fits. A
ratio near $1$ means the model isn't just \emph{fitting} the helix,
it's \emph{using} it; a ratio near $0$ means the basis fits little
that the activations contain.

\paragraph{Provenance ratio $\rho$.} The core measurement:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rho}
\rho \;=\; \frac{R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L\!=\!0)}{\max_L R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}(L)}.
\end{equation}
The numerator and denominator answer different questions about the
same cell. The denominator is the helix score K\&T's analysis
already reports: how well the trig basis fits at the model's most
helix-friendly layer. The numerator scores the same basis at
$L=0$, the residual stream \emph{before any transformer block has
run}---the output of token embedding, positional embedding, and
mean-pooling over the numeral's sub-tokens. Convention note: our
$L=0$ is HuggingFace \texttt{hidden\_states[0]}, the pre-block
embedding+pooling output. This is one step \emph{before} K\&T's
``following layer 0'' residual stream, which already has one block of
computation applied; the pre-block measurement is what we want for
the provenance question because it isolates the input pipeline. So
$\rho$ asks: ``of the helix you measure at the peak layer, how much
was already supplied by the input pipeline before the transformer
ran?'' $\rho = 1$ means
all of it (inherited); $\rho \to 0$ means depth genuinely builds the
geometry (depth-built); intermediate $\rho$ means depth amplifies a
real but partial L=0 head start.

Two notes on what $\rho$ is not. First, $\rho$ alone is insufficient:
in a cell where the basis fits little anywhere, a low $\rho$ just
means ``ratio of two small numbers,'' not ``hidden depth-built helix.''
We therefore require a quality threshold alongside $\rho$. We call a
cell \emph{depth-built} when $\rho \le 0.55$ \emph{and}
$R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}} \ge 0.70$;
\emph{depth-amplified} when $0.55 < \rho \le 0.70$ at quality $\ge
0.60$; \emph{inherited} when $\rho \ge 0.80$ at quality $\ge 0.55$;
\emph{weak} when quality $< 0.55$; \emph{ambiguous} between
thresholds. Second, $\rho$ is silent about \emph{what} depth does
when it does work---two cells can share the same $\rho$ while depth
treats their L=0 geometry very differently. The CKA check below
recovers that distinction.

\paragraph{Basis bandwidth and window wrap.} The trig basis is not
universal; it can only fit periods it explicitly includes. A model
might encode integers using a period $T$ the basis doesn't contain,
and the score will look low through no fault of the model. Two
failure modes follow.

\emph{Mode 1 (basis bandwidth):} if the script's natural period
isn't in $\mathcal{T}$, the basis is blind to it. This is analogous
to trying to detect a 1\,kHz tone with a microphone that only
captures 5--20\,kHz: the tone is there, the microphone reports ``no
signal,'' and that is a fact about the microphone, not the room.

\emph{Mode 2 (window wrap):} even if we put $T = 60$ in the basis,
$n_{\max} = 100$ wraps $T = 60$ only once (around $a = 60$). One wrap
is not enough to identify a periodic component. The Fourier
intuition: the FFT of a length-100 signal at frequency $f = 1/60$
doesn't form a clean single bin because $100$ isn't an integer
multiple of $60$. The energy of a true $T = 60$ component spreads
across multiple frequency bins (``spectral leakage''), making it
indistinguishable from noise. With ten cycles in 600 samples,
leakage is small and the peak at $1/60$ is sharp and unambiguous.
The regression intuition is the same in different language: with one
wrap of $T = 60$ over 100 samples, the columns $\cos(2\pi a/60)$ and
$\sin(2\pi a/60)$ are highly correlated with all sorts of other
columns and with each other; OLS can fit ``something'' but the
result is unstable and not attributable to $T = 60$ structure
specifically. Rule of thumb: you need the input range to wrap any
candidate period at least 8--10 times for clean identification.

Both modes hide real structure. Babylonian cuneiform (native $T =
60$), binary (natural periods $T \in \{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$), and hex
(natural periods $T \in \{16,32,64,256\}$) all fail at least one bar
at paper defaults. The mitigation is conservative: widen $n_{\max}$
past several wraps of any candidate period, and explicitly add the
script's native period to $\mathcal{T}$. If the score jumps with the
fix, the original measurement was hiding real structure; if it
doesn't, the script genuinely has no helix.

\paragraph{Random-embedding control.} The most direct test of whether
the L=0 helix is mechanical. Replace the model's learned embedding
matrix with $\tilde{E} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{d})$ at matched
per-coordinate scale, recompute the L=0 representation (the tokenizer,
the renderer, and mean-pooling are unchanged; only the embedding
lookup is replaced), and re-fit the helix. The gap
$\Delta R^2_{L=0} = R^2_{\mathrm{learned}} - R^2_{\mathrm{random}}$
is what learned embeddings contribute on top of the rendering+pooling
floor. A small $\Delta$ means the L=0 geometry is mechanical: the
embedding table contributed nothing the input pipeline didn't already
produce. A large $\Delta$ means the embedding table carries learned
structure beyond what the rendering+pooling mechanically generates.
This is the strongest version of the input-pipeline check, and the
one that gives the paper's cleanest single number
(\S\ref{sec:results-bab}).

\paragraph{Representation alignment (CKA).} The first three
measurements characterise the \emph{score} a cell achieves and where
that score lives. They are silent about \emph{shape}. Two cells can
share the same high $\rho$---both with most of the helix already at
L=0---while one of them lets the L=0 geometry pass through unchanged
and the other replaces it with a substantially different
integer-to-integer pattern that just happens to fit the trig basis
equally well. Linear CKA \citep{kornblith2019similarity} between the
helix-projected L=0 and peak-layer representations distinguishes the
two cases. Letting $X = B W^{(L=0)}$ and $Y = B W^{(\text{peak})}$,
$\mathrm{CKA}(X,Y) \in [0,1]$ measures whether the
\emph{integer-to-integer distance pattern} is the same at the two
depths. $\mathrm{CKA} \approx 1$ at high $\rho$ means
\emph{pass-through}: the input pipeline supplies the geometry and
depth doesn't touch it. $\mathrm{CKA} \ll 1$ at high $\rho$ means
\emph{rebuild}: depth replaces the L=0 helix with a similar-score
but differently-shaped one. CKA is invariant to rotation and
rescaling in feature space, so ``different geometry'' here means the
\emph{row-similarity pattern} differs, not necessarily that the
hidden directions are orthogonal.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Models} (all base, non-instruction-tuned): Pythia-6.9B,
GPT-J-6B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-4-E4B, Gemma-4-31B, OLMo-3-32B,
Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B---a superset of the K\&T model set (Pythia,
GPT-J, Llama) chosen to cover three architectural axes the original
paper does not: a multilingual family with strong Indic/Arabic-Indic
exposure (Gemma 4), a model family that aggressively splits numbers
into per-digit tokens (Qwen 2.5), and a recent base model with a
well-documented training mix (OLMo-3). Sizes range from 4\,B
(Gemma-4-E4B) to 32\,B (Qwen2.5-32B, OLMo-3-32B, Gemma-4-31B). Layer
counts range from 28 (Pythia, GPT-J) to 64 (OLMo, Qwen2.5-32B); the
helix's peak layer varies enormously across this set (App.~\ref{app:layers}).

\textbf{Scripts} (12 total, four families); Table~\ref{tab:scripts}
gives each script's rendering of 23 and its mathematical family.
\textbf{Pooling.} K\&T focus on the \emph{single-token regime}
``for simplicity''---GPT-J tokenizes integers in $[0, 361]$ as one
token, Pythia $[0, 557]$, Llama $[0, 999]$---so for Pythia/GPT-J/Llama
on Latin at $n_{\max}=100$, every integer is one token and the
choice of pool is moot. Outside that regime the choice matters:
Qwen and Gemma split Latin numbers per-digit even at $n=100$, every
non-Latin script is multi-token on every tokenizer, and our
extended-basis Babylonian/binary/hex runs operate well outside any
model's single-token range. We use mean pooling throughout, which is
the honest extension---last-token pooling produces a period-10
artifact because numbers $23, 33, 43, \ldots$ all share the same
final sub-token in per-digit BPE; mean pooling is also the channel
through which the rendering-only structure on Babylonian
(\S\ref{sec:results-bab}) becomes visible. The default protocol is
$n_{\max}=100$, $\mathcal{T}=\{2,5,10,100\}$; Babylonian/binary/hex
additionally run with wider windows and native bases
(App.~\ref{app:bases}).

For each $(\text{model}, \text{script})$ cell we run one forward
pass per integer with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states}, mean-pool the
numeral's sub-token positions at every layer, fit the helix basis,
and record per-layer $R^2$ and $\rho$. Inputs are space-prefixed
(``\texttt{\textvisiblespace 23}'' rather than bare ``\texttt{23}''),
matching K\&T's convention---GPT-style tokenizers can split
\texttt{"23"} and \texttt{" 23"} into different tokens, and natural
prompts almost always have a leading space or operator. Code, configurations, and
all 8 GB of intermediate JSON are public; see App.~\ref{app:repro}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The paper-models / Latin cells survive cleanly}
\label{sec:results-paper}

The first check is whether our measurements reproduce the original
paper's finding on the cells where the original protocol is the
right one. On K\&T's three models reading Latin digits, $\rho$ is
well below the inherited threshold and quality is high: Pythia
$\rho=0.34$ ($R^2_{\mathrm{helix}}/R^2_{\mathrm{PCA}}=0.85$), Llama
$\rho=0.39$ ($0.85$), GPT-J $\rho=0.41$ ($0.83$). Less than half of
the helix is at L=0; the rest is genuinely built by depth. All
three cells clear the depth-built bar comfortably, and the
random-embedding control on Latin (App.~\ref{app:embed-control})
shows the L=0 helix on these models is already substantially
learned: random embeddings of matched scale recover only $R^2
\approx 0.08$ at L=0 (essentially chance) compared to the learned
$0.18{-}0.26$, leaving $\Delta R^2 = +0.10$ to $+0.15$ for the
embedding contribution alone. The K\&T result is intact on the
cells where they claimed it.

\emph{Where the helix lives in the stack.} K\&T originally read
at the middle layer ($n_{\mathrm{layers}} // 2$). For Pythia (32
layers), that is L=16; for Llama (32 layers), L=16. Both have peaks
near there, so the middle-layer heuristic was fine for K\&T's three
models. It is the wrong heuristic for any of the five models we
add. Pythia's actual peak is at L=32 of 32 (the literal final
layer); GPT-J's at L=28 of 28; Llama's at L=15 of 32 (close to the
middle); Qwen2.5-7B/Latin peaks at L=0, before any block has run
(the helix is complete in the embedding+pooling output);
Qwen2.5-32B/Latin peaks at L=3 of 64; Gemma-4-31B holds a moderate
helix R² across most of the stack with a sharp spike to peak at
L=29 of 60; Gemma-4-E4B has a double-peaked structure with global
maximum at L=17 of 42; OLMo-3-32B rises gradually to peak at L=23
of 64. Without the layer sweep we could not compute $\rho$ at all
(there would be no $\max_L R^2$ to ratio L=0 against), and the
sweep is also what lets us target our standard figures (FFT, PC1,
circles, 3D helix) at each model's actual peak rather than at
K\&T's middle-layer default. Per-model curves are in
App.~\ref{app:layers}.

\subsection{Cross-script generalisation is mostly pre-transformer}
\label{sec:results-cross}

A natural next step is to ask whether the helix story generalises
beyond the cells K\&T tested. The raw helix matrix \emph{looks} like
it does: many non-Latin cells fit the trig basis non-trivially. The
provenance check shows that most of that apparent generalisation is
pre-transformer.

Figure~\ref{fig:rho-heatmap} shows $\rho$ for all 96 cells at the
paper-default protocol. The cool (depth-built) row is visually narrow:
outside the three paper-model Latin cells, depth substantially
contributes only on Gemma's strong non-Latin cells (Devanagari,
Persian, Roman; $\rho = 0.44{-}0.63$)---scripts where Gemma 4
plausibly had heavy training exposure, though we do not measure
pre-training distributions directly. Everything else outside Latin
is warm. Most non-Latin cells on Pythia and Llama sit at
$\rho = 0.73{-}1.00$: the helix is already present at $L=0$ because
the tokenizer splits e.g.\ \arabicnum{٢٣} into per-digit sub-tokens
whose mean (over the two positions) varies smoothly with the digit
values. The trig basis fits that smooth variation as a helix without
any depth being involved.

Two models take pre-transformer geometry to its limit even on Latin.
Qwen2.5-7B/Latin has $\rho = 1.00$ exactly: the helix peaks at the
literal embedding output before any block has run. Qwen2.5-32B/Latin
peaks at L3 of 64 ($\rho = 0.89$). On Qwen 2.5 the Latin cell that
K\&T's reading would call depth-built on Pythia/Llama is instead
near-pure inheritance---a function of how Qwen's tokenizer breaks
Latin numbers and how mean-pooling combines the sub-tokens.

Greek alphabetic numerals are uniformly weak ($\rho \ge 0.94$ on
every model that does not actively train on Greek): Greek 23 is
\greeknum{κγ}, two letters from disjoint alphabet blocks with no
positional period-10 structure. There is no helix to inherit and no
helix for depth to build. The mutually-exclusive classifier of

depth-amplified at paper defaults---about 10\% of the matrix. The
helix framing borrows rhetorical strength from the three paper-model
Latin cells to characterise cells where the actual mechanism is
quite different.

\subsection{Babylonian: geometry from rendering alone}
\label{sec:results-bab}

Babylonian cuneiform is positional at base 60 (additive within each
sexagesimal column) and so fails both spectral checks at the paper
defaults: $T = 60$ is not in $\mathcal{T} = \{2,5,10,100\}$, and a
window of $n_{\max} = 100$ wraps $T = 60$ only once. The natural
protocol fix---widen $n_{\max}$ to 600 and add $T = 60$ to
$\mathcal{T}$---recovers $8{-}16$ percentage points of helix $R^2$
uniformly across all 8 models, with the largest gain on Qwen2.5-7B
(+0.16). The basis-free FFT spectrum at the wider window confirms
peaks at multiples of $1/60$. A reading focused on the score would
stop here: cuneiform on every model carries period-60 structure once
you measure properly.

The random-embedding control reframes that conclusion sharply. With
the native protocol fixed, we run the control of
\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}: replace the model's embedding table with a
matched-scale Gaussian random matrix and recompute the L=0 helix
score. Table~\ref{tab:bab-rand} reports the learned-vs-random gap.
The maximum absolute $|\Delta|$ across all eight models is $0.0073$;
two models have negative $\Delta$ (the random fit is marginally
\emph{better} than the learned one). The L=0 Babylonian helix is
not learned: it is mechanical.

The mechanism is direct. Walking through one concrete integer makes
it explicit. The pipeline that produces $h(n)$ at $L=0$ is the same
four steps for every cell:
\emph{rendering $\to$ tokenization $\to$ embedding lookup $\to$
mean-pooling}. The random-embedding control swaps the third step
only. For $n = 23$ on Babylonian:

\emph{Step 1 (rendering).}
\texttt{format\_number(23, ``babylonian'')} returns
\cuneinum{𒌋𒌋𒁹𒁹𒁹}: two ten-wedges followed by three one-wedges.
The structure is additive---the number of \cuneinum{𒌋} equals the
tens digit, the number of \cuneinum{𒁹} equals the ones digit.

\emph{Step 2 (tokenization).} The space-prefixed string is fed to
the tokenizer. Cuneiform was not in any of our eight models'
pre-training corpora to speak of, so each codepoint falls back to
its UTF-8 byte representation (four bytes per cuneiform codepoint,
often each becoming its own sub-token). Crucially, every
\cuneinum{𒌋} expands to the same fixed byte-token sequence, and
likewise for \cuneinum{𒁹}. Writing $T_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}$ for the
byte-tokens of one \cuneinum{𒌋}, the tokenisation of 23 is
$[\text{space},\, T_{\cuneinum{𒌋}},\, T_{\cuneinum{𒌋}},\,
T_{\cuneinum{𒁹}},\, T_{\cuneinum{𒁹}},\, T_{\cuneinum{𒁹}}]$. The
count structure of step 1 is preserved.

\emph{Step 3 (embedding lookup).} Each token ID indexes a row of the
embedding matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}$. Write
$e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}$ for the contribution of one
$T_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}$ (the sum of the byte-token rows). The result is
the sequence $[e_{\text{space}},\, e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}},\,
e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}},\, e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}},\, e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}},\,
e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}}]$.

\emph{Step 4 (mean-pooling).} Average:
\begin{equation}
h(23) \;\approx\; \frac{e_{\text{space}} + 2\, e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}
  + 3\, e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}}}{6}.
\end{equation}
This is a linear combination of three \emph{fixed} vectors with
coefficients that come from the renderer's count structure: 2 = the
tens digit of 23, 3 = the ones digit. Generalising for $n < 60$,
\begin{equation}
h(n) \;\approx\; \alpha(n)\, e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}} + \beta(n)\, e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}} + \text{constant},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha(n) = \lfloor n/10 \rfloor$ and $\beta(n) = n \bmod 10$.
Both are smooth/periodic functions of $n$; the trig basis with
$T \in \{10, 60\}$ in $\mathcal{T}$ fits the coefficient structure.

\emph{What the random-embedding control changes.} Steps 1, 2, 4 are
unchanged. Only step 3 changes: $e_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}$ and
$e_{\cuneinum{𒁹}}$ are replaced by independent draws from
$\mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{d})$. So
\begin{equation}
\tilde{h}(n) \;\approx\; \alpha(n)\, \tilde{e}_{\cuneinum{𒌋}} + \beta(n)\, \tilde{e}_{\cuneinum{𒁹}} + \text{constant}.
\end{equation}
The vectors $\tilde{e}_{\cuneinum{𒌋}}$ and
$\tilde{e}_{\cuneinum{𒁹}}$ now point in random directions in
$\mathbb{R}^{d}$, but they are still \emph{fixed} vectors, and the
coefficients $\alpha(n)$ and $\beta(n)$ are the same smooth
functions of $n$. The trig basis fits the coefficient structure
regardless of which directions the random vectors point. The fitted
helix \emph{rotates} in residual space with each random seed; the
fit \emph{quality} doesn't change. This is the algebra behind
Table~\ref{tab:bab-rand}.

The contrast with Latin/Pythia is sharp. Pythia tokenizes ``\,23'' as
a single token, so the chain collapses to
$h(23) = e_{23}$. The helix R² is asking whether the 100 learned
embeddings $\{e_0, e_1, \ldots, e_{99}\}$ themselves lie on a helix.
With learned Pythia weights they do; with random embeddings they
don't, because 100 independent random vectors carry no inter-integer
structure. So the same swap-embeddings control gives sharply
different verdicts on the two cells---and the difference is fully
explained by which step in the four-step chain carries the
geometry.

This is the strongest example of the warning Singh \& Strouse's
behavioral result implies at the representational level: tokenization
is not a neutral preprocessing step but an inductive bias in the
numerical reasoning pipeline. On Babylonian, the bias is so strong
that it generates the entire L=0 helix score on its own.

\subsection{Pass-through versus rebuild}
\label{sec:results-cka}

$\rho$ alone conflates two mechanistically distinct high-$\rho$
regimes. The CKA check from \S\ref{sec:diagnostic} separates them.
A concrete comparison makes the distinction sharp.

\emph{Two cells with similar $\rho$.} Qwen2.5-32B/Latin and
Gemma-4-31B/Latin both have high $\rho$ ($0.89$ and $0.79$):
on both cells, the helix score at the peak layer is mostly already
at $L=0$. A $\rho$-only reading puts them in the same bucket:
``inherited; the transformer doesn't do much.'' Now look at CKA on
the helix-projected representations: Qwen-32B/Latin has $\text{CKA}
= 0.91$, Gemma-31B/Latin has $\text{CKA} = 0.33$. Same $\rho$, very
different CKA.

\emph{What that means geometrically.} Imagine the 3D helix at $L=0$
with integers labelled, then the 3D helix at the peak layer (same
model, same script). For Qwen-32B, the two plots are nearly
identical: integer 23 is in the same position on the helix in both,
integer 47 is in the same position, and so on. The transformer left
the arrangement alone---\emph{pass-through}. For Gemma-31B, the two
plots have the same overall helical shape, but the labels have
moved: integer 23 might be at the back of the L=0 helix and at the
front of the peak helix. The score is the same (both fit the trig
basis equally well), but it's a \emph{different} helix---the
row-similarity pattern across integers has changed. This is
\emph{rebuild}.

Linear CKA captures exactly this distinction. It is invariant to
rotation and rescaling of the feature columns, so it isn't asking
``are the two helices in the same place in residual space?''---they
obviously aren't, depth has done work. It's asking ``is the
row-similarity pattern the same?''---i.e., are the same integers
close to each other in both helices? CKA $\approx 1$ on Qwen-32B
says yes; CKA $\approx 0.33$ on Gemma-31B says no.

\emph{Three patterns across the matrix.}
\emph{Pass-through} (high $\rho$, high CKA): Qwen2.5-32B/Latin
($0.89$, $0.91$), OLMo/Latin ($0.63$, $0.91$), most Pythia and
Llama non-Latin positional cells ($\text{CKA} \ge 0.98$ on Persian,
Devanagari, Chinese on Pythia). The input pipeline does all the
work and the transformer passes the geometry through.
\emph{Rebuild} (high $\rho$, low CKA): the entire Gemma family
($\text{CKA} = 0.21{-}0.65$ across most scripts), and a third
pattern that recovers Babylonian's full story. On
Pythia/Llama/OLMo, Babylonian has $\rho \approx 0.86$ but
$\text{CKA} = 0.25{-}0.53$: the peak Babylonian helix is depth-built
\emph{on top of} a mechanical L=0 starting point. The score doesn't
change much (the mechanical L=0 is already healthy), but depth
rebuilds the integer-to-integer pattern. What that rebuilt helix is
computing is a separate, causal question---one $\rho + \text{CKA}$
flags but doesn't answer.

The full $(\rho, \text{CKA})$ scatter and a four-quadrant
breakdown are in App.~\ref{app:cka}.

\subsection{Binary and hex: two more case studies in basis bandwidth}
\label{sec:results-bases}

Babylonian is not the only script where the paper basis is the wrong
basis. Binary's natural periods are powers of two ($T \in
\{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$), of which $\mathcal{T} = \{2,5,10,100\}$
catches only $T = 2$; hexadecimal's natural periods are $T \in
\{16,32,64,256\}$, none of which paper basis catches. Running both
at $n_{\max} = 1024$ with the native basis recovers $+0.10$ to
$+0.37$ helix $R^2$ on binary and $+0.19$ to $+0.29$ on hex
(App.~\ref{app:bases}). The protocol fix is the same; the
mechanistic readings on the two scripts are entirely different.

\emph{Binary is mechanical}, like Babylonian. The random-embedding
gap with the binary-native basis is $|\Delta R^2| \le 0.03$ on every
model: the L=0 helix on binary is generated by the renderer (binary
strings have length $\log_2 n$, whose count varies with $n$) and the
tokenizer (BPE chunks of bits) interacting with mean-pooling.
Pythia/Llama/GPT-J/OLMo carry moderately-low native-basis $\rho$ of
$0.62{-}0.77$ on binary, but this reflects depth doing additional
work on top of a mechanical L=0, not learned bit semantics; the
corresponding CKA values ($0.55{-}0.71$) confirm depth amplifies
rather than rebuilds. Binary is the second mechanical case in the
matrix.

\emph{Hex is depth-built}, the second-cleanest depth-built case in
the matrix. Native-basis $\rho$ on Pythia/Llama/GPT-J/OLMo is
$0.29, 0.30, 0.35, 0.35$---squarely in the same regime as the
original paper's Latin cells ($\rho = 0.34, 0.39, 0.41$). The
random-embedding gap is positive on every model (small on
Llama/OLMo, sizeable on Gemma), so hex digit embeddings do carry
learned structure that random vectors don't reproduce. The natural
hypothesis is that all these models saw substantial hex during
pre-training (code, addresses, hashes), but the data here doesn't
directly test pre-training distributions. Whatever the cause, on the
four models with the cleanest result, the model is treating hex
digits the way it treats Latin digits: as indices into a value space,
with layer-by-layer construction of the same kind of helix.

The general pattern across the three case studies: each non-decimal
base needs its own protocol fix before its $\rho$ becomes
interpretable, and once the fix is applied, the rest of the
measurements cleanly separate ``mechanical inheritance'' (Babylonian,
binary) from ``depth-built'' (hex on the paper models) within the
same broad failure mode.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The reframing is the contribution, not the matrix. Three problems
can make the standard ``fit a helix, report $R^2$'' procedure answer
the wrong question. Two of them hide real structure: the basis can
miss the period the model actually uses (\S\ref{sec:results-bab},
\S\ref{sec:results-bases}), and the window can be too short to
identify a real period (Babylonian at $n_{\max} = 100$). The third
credits the transformer with structure it did not build---the case
where the helix score is high because rendering, tokenization,
embedding lookup, and mean-pooling already produced the geometry.
$\rho$ catches this, and the random-embedding control isolates how
much of the L=0 geometry is mechanical versus learned. CKA then
reveals a fourth: a high $\rho$ does not always mean the geometry
is untouched. Sometimes depth rebuilds the integer-to-integer
pattern with similar quality but different shape, and a $\rho$-only
analysis would lump that case in with genuine pass-through.

Across the matrix, the cells where K\&T's reading is unambiguously
correct are narrower than the generalisation suggests: 6 of 96 cells
at paper defaults clear the joint $\rho$ + quality bar for
depth-built or depth-amplified geometry, concentrated on the three
paper-model Latin cells and Gemma's strong non-Latin scripts
(App.~\ref{app:classification}). Outside that narrow set, the
apparent cross-script generalisation of the helix is mostly
pre-transformer.

The Babylonian random-embedding result is the sharpest version of a
general warning: \emph{some apparent neural geometry can come from
rendering statistics alone}, before learned embeddings or
transformer blocks are even needed. Smooth,
low-dimensional, easy-to-fit manifolds can be artefacts of how the
input is encoded. For number helices specifically, $\rho + \text{CKA}$ together with
the two spectral checks are how we asked the provenance question.
The general lesson is independent of the specific measurements: for
other geometric claims---probe-discovered features,
sparse-autoencoder directions, ``the model represents $X$ as a
manifold''---the right baseline is not zero or noise; it is the
geometry the input pipeline already produces with random embeddings.

The reframing does not invalidate the existing literature. K\&T's
Latin results survive cleanly, and the replication of the helix
across eight models on Latin shows it is an architecturally general
object even if its provenance is not. Babylonian's base-60 result
is then a substantive empirical statement about input
pipelines---an additive symbolic renderer composed with mean-pooling
is enough to produce a measurable helix. Causal work like
\citet{feucht2026wild}, which finds a base-10 addition module in
Llama-3.1-8B reading exactly the Fourier features the trig basis
fits, lands on exactly the cell our measurements mark as
depth-built: the natural follow-up is to apply that kind of causal
interrogation to the cells where $\rho + \text{CKA}$ flag a high
score with no depth-built helix underneath.

\section{Methods details}
\label{app:methods}

\paragraph{Activation collection.} For each integer $a$, we feed
``\texttt{<bos> {\normalfont format\_number}($a$, script)}'' through
the model with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True} and pool the
numeral's sub-token positions at every layer. We use mean pooling
throughout; last-token pooling produces a period-10 artefact for
multi-token scripts because numbers $23, 33, 43, \ldots$ all share
the same last sub-token in per-digit BPE.

\paragraph{Helix fit.} For numbers $\{0, \ldots, n_{\max}-1\}$ and
periods $\mathcal{T}$, we construct $B$ of shape
$(n_{\max}, 1 + 2|\mathcal{T}|)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:basis}, then solve
$H \approx B W$ by ordinary least squares with intercept. Report
variance-weighted $R^2$. The Eckart--Young upper bound is the
variance-weighted reconstruction $R^2$ of the best $(1 + 2|\mathcal{T}|)$-d
PCA of $H$; the helix/PCA ratio is our quality metric.

\paragraph{$\rho$ and the peak layer.} We collect activations at every
\texttt{hidden\_states[L]} (one forward pass per $a$), so $L=0$ is the
embedding output (after the token + positional embeddings and pooling,
before any transformer block). We compute the helix $R^2$ at every
$L$ and define $\rho$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:rho}.

\paragraph{Random-embedding control.} For a model with embedding
matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}$ we draw
$\tilde{E} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/\sqrt{d})$ (matched per-coordinate
scale), use the model's actual tokenizer to obtain sub-token id
sequences for each $a$, mean-pool $\tilde{E}$ rows over those
sub-tokens to obtain $\tilde{h}(a)$, fit the helix on $\tilde{H}$, and
compare $\tilde{R}^2$ to the learned $R^2_{L=0}$. The tokenizer +
renderer + pooling are identical to the learned condition; only the
embedding lookup is replaced. No transformer blocks are run.

\paragraph{Linear CKA.} For mean-centred $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_x}$,
$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_y}$,
$\mathrm{CKA}(X,Y) = \|X^\top Y\|_F^2 \,/\, (\|X^\top X\|_F \cdot \|Y^\top Y\|_F).$
We report CKA on the helix-projected representations
$X = B W^{(L\!=\!0)}$, $Y = B W^{(\text{peak})}$, isolating whether
the \emph{helix slice} of the residual stream is the same at the two
depths, not whether the rest of the model state is.

\paragraph{Classification thresholds.} Mutually exclusive criteria:
\emph{depth-built} requires $\rho \le 0.55$ \emph{and} helix/PCA $\ge 0.70$;
\emph{depth-amplified} requires $0.55 < \rho \le 0.70$ and helix/PCA
$\ge 0.60$; \emph{inherited} requires $\rho \ge 0.80$ at helix/PCA
$\ge 0.55$; \emph{weak} is helix/PCA $< 0.55$; \emph{ambiguous} is
everything else (e.g.\ $0.70 < \rho < 0.80$). The bar is deliberately
strict; relaxing helix/PCA to $0.60$ adds Gemma's two Roman cells.

\section{Per-model peak layers on Latin}
\label{app:layers}

\textbf{Where the helix lives varies dramatically across models.}
Pythia ($n_L = 32$) rises monotonically and peaks at the literal
final layer. GPT-J ($n_L = 28$) does the same. Llama-3.1-8B ($n_L
= 32$) peaks mid-stack at L=15. Gemma-4-E4B ($n_L = 42$) shows two
regimes with a broad peak at L=17. Gemma-4-31B ($n_L = 60$) holds
$\sim 0.5$ across most of the stack with a sharp spike to $0.70$ at
L=29. OLMo-3-32B ($n_L = 64$) rises through mid-stack to a peak at
L=23. Qwen2.5-7B/Latin peaks at the literal embedding output
(L=0; the helix is complete \emph{before any block has run}).
Qwen2.5-32B peaks at L=3 of 64. ``Read the middle layer'' is a
Pythia-ism; for any new model, a layer sweep is mandatory before
defining $\rho$.

\section{$(\rho, \text{CKA})$ scatter and the four quadrants}
\label{app:cka}

We compute linear CKA between the helix-projected L=0 representation
and the helix-projected peak-layer representation for every cell. Four
quadrants emerge in the $(\rho, \text{CKA})$ plane.

\textbf{Pass-through} (high $\rho$, high CKA): Qwen2.5-32B/Latin
($\rho=0.89$, $\text{CKA}=0.91$), OLMo/Latin ($0.63$, $0.91$),
Pythia $\times$ Persian/Devanagari/Chinese ($\text{CKA} \ge 0.98$).
\textbf{Depth refines} (low-to-moderate $\rho$, high CKA):
Pythia/Latin ($0.34$, $0.79$), Llama/Latin ($0.39$, $0.81$), Roman on
every non-Gemma model ($\text{CKA} = 0.77{-}0.90$).
\textbf{Rebuild} (high $\rho$, low CKA): the entire Gemma family
($\text{CKA} = 0.21{-}0.65$ across most scripts), Babylonian on
Pythia/Llama/OLMo ($\rho \approx 0.86$, $\text{CKA} = 0.25{-}0.53$),
Qwen on Devanagari ($\text{CKA} \approx 0.24{-}0.30$).
\textbf{Depth builds new} (low $\rho$, low CKA): rare; Gemma's
Devanagari/Persian/Roman/Greek cells edge into it
($\rho \approx 0.44{-}0.63$, $\text{CKA} \approx 0.29{-}0.57$).

The pass-through / rebuild distinction is the key new fact CKA
contributes. The simpler reading---``Gemma/Latin $\rho \approx 0.85$,
so mostly tokenizer''---was wrong. CKA on the same cell is only
$0.33$; depth produces a similar-quality helix with a substantially
different integer-to-integer geometry. Across nearly every script,
Gemma rebuilds. $\rho$ alone made it look like Gemma was doing
nothing; it is doing a lot, just not in a way that changes the score.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

All code is public at
\url{https://github.com/girish/addition_helix} (final URL on
publication). Each driver script declares its dependencies via PEP 723
inline metadata and is runnable directly via \texttt{uv run}; one
top-level \texttt{run.sh} executes the per-cell sweep
(\texttt{main.py}), the aggregator (\texttt{aggregate.py}), the
random-embedding control (\texttt{embed\_control.py}), and the
representation-alignment pass (\texttt{subspace\_align.py}). The
companion blogpost site (\texttt{www/}) reads the aggregated JSON
and renders all figures interactively.

\paragraph{Compute.} The full 96-cell sweep plus the three auxiliary
passes completes in 6--8 hours on a single Apple M5 Max with 128\,GB
unified memory (MPS); fallback CUDA/CPU paths are in
\texttt{main.py:pick\_device}. The 32B-class models (Gemma-4-31B,
OLMo-3-32B, Qwen2.5-32B) run in bf16. Each combination is idempotent:
re-running skips any cell whose \texttt{fig\_layer\_sweep.json}
already exists.

\paragraph{Data.} All inputs are integers; we render them via
\texttt{format\_number} in \texttt{helix\_lib.py} (12 scripts) and
feed them through each model's own tokenizer. No external corpus is
used.
